Quick Answer

Use the holidays to notice, listen and start a gentle conversation, not to make every decision. Agree on one next step, set aside the things that matter to the family, and plan the bigger work for the new year. Clearing a full house usually takes about two months from the first meeting, so a January start fits a spring move.

For many families, the holidays are the first time in months that everyone is back in Mom and Dad's house at the same time. You walk in and notice things a phone call never shows you: the stairs are harder, the mail is piling up, whole rooms have stopped being used. It's a tender time to notice, and a good time to start talking.

It's also when families make up their minds. In each of the last two winters, more families started an estate sale conversation with us in January than in the quieter December before it. This guide is for the visit itself: what to look for, how to bring it up, and what comes next.

What to Notice During Your Visit

You're not there to inspect the house. But a few signs are worth noticing quietly:

  • Stairs and steps that have become hard, or a bedroom moved downstairs
  • Rooms closed off or used only for storage
  • Mail, bills or prescriptions piling up
  • A refrigerator with expired food, or very little food
  • Upkeep slipping: the yard, gutters, small repairs left undone
  • Clutter that gets in the way of walking safely, not just a lifetime of collecting
  • What your parents say themselves: "This place is too much," or "I'd like to be closer to the grandkids"

One sign on its own may mean nothing. Several together usually mean it's time to talk. If you're worried about health or safety, talk with their doctor as well.

How to Start the Conversation

  1. Pick a calm moment. A walk or a quiet coffee works better than the holiday dinner table.
  2. Ask, don't tell. "How is the house feeling these days?" opens a door. "You need to move" closes one.
  3. Start with what they want. Stay home? Something smaller? Closer to family? Help nearby? Their answer shapes everything else.
  4. Talk about the belongings early. Often the house isn't the hard part; it's a lifetime of things. Knowing there's a plan for them makes the move feel possible.
  5. Bring your siblings in. Share what you noticed with brothers and sisters, especially those who couldn't come home this year.
  6. Agree on one next step, not the whole plan. "Let's have someone walk through the house in January" is enough for now.

What If Mom or Dad Says No?

Many parents say no the first time. That's normal, and it's their home. A few things help:

  • Ask what worries them. Often it's losing independence, leaving memories behind, or the sheer size of the job.
  • Make the home safer in the meantime. Better lighting, grab bars and clear walkways help whatever they decide.
  • Leave the door open and come back to it. A plan they help shape is one they'll follow.

Use the Holidays to Sort What Matters

The holidays may be the only time the whole family is in the house together. That makes it the best time to talk about the things that matter most, and the worst time to try to clear the house.

  • Walk through with your parents and let them tell the stories behind the pieces they love. Write them down or record them.
  • Find the irreplaceable things: photos, letters, recipes, military papers, family records.
  • Ask who would like what. Use tape or a shared list so nobody is surprised later.
  • Don't throw things out yet. Items families assume are worthless sometimes sell well. Let someone who knows values look first.

A Downsizing Checklist for Your Holiday Visit

  • ☐ Notice how your parents move through the house: stairs, bathrooms, entries
  • ☐ Ask what they want for the next few years, in their words
  • ☐ Note which rooms are used every day and which aren't
  • ☐ Find where important papers live: will or trust, deed, insurance, medical contacts
  • ☐ Walk through together and record the stories behind favorite pieces
  • ☐ Make a list of family keepsakes and who would like them
  • ☐ Take photos of each room to share with siblings who couldn't come
  • ☐ Agree on who leads the plan in the family
  • ☐ Agree on one next step for January
  • ☐ Don't throw out anything of possible value until it has been looked at

Three Paths After the Holidays

1. Stay home, safely

If your parents want to stay, the house may need changes: fewer stairs, safer bathrooms, better lighting, less to maintain. Clearing out rooms they no longer use often helps more than people expect.

2. Move somewhere smaller, or closer to family

A smaller home or a move near grandchildren means choosing what comes along, and deciding what happens to everything else and to the house. An estate sale can clear the rest in one organized weekend instead of months of trips.

3. Move to assisted living or memory care

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the right time to talk to my parents about moving?
Earlier than feels necessary, and when nothing is urgent. A calm talk over the holidays, when you can see the house for yourself, is far easier than a rushed one after a fall or a hospital stay.
What do we do with everything they can't take with them?
Family takes what matters first. An estate sale usually sells most of the rest, and what doesn't sell is donated or hauled away. Don't throw things out before someone who knows values has looked.
Do we have to sell the house?
No. Some families keep it, rent it or let a sibling move in. If it does need to be sold, it can be listed with an agent or sold as-is for cash. The belongings are a separate decision.
How long does it take to clear a parent's house?
For the estate sales True Legacy Homes signed in 2026, the typical family signed about two weeks after the first meeting and held the sale about three weeks after that. Plan on roughly five weeks from the first meeting to the sale.
What if my brothers and sisters disagree?
That's common. Agree on who makes the final call, put the plan in writing, and share photos of the house with anyone who can't be there. An outside company can also take the pressure off decisions about pricing and timing.
